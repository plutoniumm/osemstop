\begin{frame}{The pendulum, and why it needs help}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
      \[ \ddot{x} + \frac{\omega_0}{Q}\,\dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = \frac{F(t)}{m} \]
      \[ \omega_0 = 2\pi f_0,\qquad f_0 \approx 1\ \mathrm{Hz},\qquad Q \approx 50 \]
      \[ \boxed{\ \tau = \frac{Q}{\pi f_0} \approx 16\ \mathrm{s}\ } \]
      \vspace{1mm}

\note{The optic hangs as a pendulum near one hertz with a quality factor around
fifty. Neither number was characterised on this bench -- Q and drive amplitude
are sliders in the simulator precisely because nobody measured them -- but they
are the working values everywhere in the repo.

\begin{frame}{Cold damping: the process variable is \emph{velocity}}
  \begin{align*}
    \mathrm{bp}(t) &= \mathrm{LP}_{3\,\mathrm{Hz}}\big[\,\mathrm{HP}_{0.4\,\mathrm{Hz}}[A(t)]\,\big] \\[2pt]
    v(t) &= \mathrm{LP}_{5\,\mathrm{Hz}}\!\left[\frac{\mathrm{bp}(t)-\mathrm{bp}(t-\Delta t)}{\Delta t}\right] \\[2pt]
    u(t) &= -K(t)\cdot v(t)
  \end{align*}
  \vspace{-2mm}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=4mm]
    \node[blk] (osem) {OSEM\\counts};
    \node[blk, right=of osem] (bp) {bandpass\\0.4--3\,Hz};
    \node[blk, right=of bp] (dv) {$d/dt$ +\\LP 5\,Hz};
    \node[blk, right=of dv] (err) {$e = -v$\\setpoint 0};
    \node[blk, right=of err] (pid) {PID\\$K_p,K_i,K_d$};
    \node[blk, right=of pid] (dac) {clip to\\0--0.5\,V};
    \node[blk, right=of dac] (coil) {coil};
    \foreach \a/\b in {osem/bp, bp/dv, dv/err, err/pid, pid/dac, dac/coil}
      \draw[flow] (\a) -- (\b);
    \draw[flow] (coil.south) -- ++(0,-0.55) -| node[pos=0.25, below, font=\scriptsize]
      {optic} (osem.south);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{1mm}
  {\footnotesize The setpoint is a constant zero, so $e=-v$: the loop does not
  ask where the optic \emph{is}, only that it stop \emph{moving}.
  \src{provenance.md, ``The documented algorithm''}}
\end{frame}
\note{This is the whole control law, and it has not changed since v2. Bandpass
the raw sensor between 0.4 and 3 hertz, differentiate to get velocity, smooth
that at 5 hertz, and drive the coil with minus a gain times velocity.

The critical thing -- and the thing that makes every textbook PID intuition
misleading here -- is that the process variable is velocity, not position, and
the setpoint is a constant zero. So the error is simply minus the velocity. The
loop has no opinion about where the optic sits; it only objects to it moving.
That is what "cold damping" means: you are synthesising a viscous drag that the
suspension deliberately does not have.

The bandpass matters too. All three of the modelled rigid-body resonances sit
inside 0.4 to 3 hertz. A resonance outside that band would be filtered out of
the very signal the loop closes on.}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 6
\begin{frame}{Three gains: P is a damper, I is a spring, D is a mass}
  \centering
  With $e = -v$ and $v = \dot{x}$:
  \[
    \underbrace{K_p\,e = -K_p\,\dot{x}}_{\text{force} \propto \text{velocity}}
    \qquad
    \underbrace{K_i\!\int\! e\,dt = -K_i\,x}_{\text{force} \propto \text{displacement}}
    \qquad
    \underbrace{K_d\,\frac{de}{dt} = -K_d\,\ddot{x}}_{\text{force} \propto \text{acceleration}}
  \]
  \vspace{2mm}
  {\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
    \toprule
    term & acts on & physically & effect on the pole \\
    \midrule
    $K_p$ & velocity & \textbf{viscous damper} & \textbf{the only term that removes energy} \\
    $K_i$ & displacement & added \textbf{spring} & moves the resonance; does not damp \\
    $K_d$ & acceleration & added (negative) \textbf{mass} & noisiest term --- differentiates twice \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}}

  \vspace{3mm}
  {\footnotesize ``Expect the amplitude ratio to end up no better than v1's,
  because neither new term dissipates. What they buy is a stiffer, heavier
  effective plant.'' \src{osem.v2.py docstring}}
\end{frame}
\note{This is the single most illuminating slide in the deck for anyone who
knows ordinary PID.

Because the error is minus velocity, every term is shifted by one derivative
from what its name suggests. P acts on velocity, so it is a viscous damper --
and it is the only one of the three that takes energy out of the pendulum.
That is the entire purpose of the loop. I integrates velocity, and the integral
of velocity is displacement, so the I term is a spring: it moves the resonance
but it does not dissipate anything. D differentiates velocity, and the
derivative of velocity is acceleration, so the D term is added mass -- and
since it has now differentiated the sensor signal twice, it is by far the
noisiest term in the loop.

v2's own docstring predicted this in advance: switching I and D on would not
damp better than P alone, it would give you a stiffer, heavier effective plant.
That prediction held -- the ladder table shows v2 at 0.028 against v1 at 0.025,
which is no improvement.}

\begin{frame}{Where the three numbers came from}
  \centering
  {\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
    \toprule
     & ch0 & ch1 & \textbf{ch2} & ch3 \\
    \midrule
    \texttt{STEADY\_GAIN}  & $-0.030$ & $-0.030$ & $\mathbf{+0.010}$ & $-0.030$ \\
    \texttt{CAPTURE\_GAIN} & $-0.035$ & $-0.035$ & $\mathbf{+0.012}$ & $-0.035$ \\
    \texttt{KI\_GAIN}      & $-0.040$ & $-0.040$ & $\mathbf{+0.040}$ & $-0.040$ \\
    \texttt{KD\_GAIN}      & $-0.0015$ & $-0.0015$ & $\mathbf{+0.0015}$ & $-0.0015$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}}

  \vspace{3mm}
  {\footnotesize
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
    \item \textbf{$-0.030$ is the only gain ever validated against a scope} ---
          ch0, 2026-07-15 run 3: ``real damping at gain $=-0.03$, 6$\times$
          reduction, no rail clipping.''
    \item \texttt{CAPTURE\_GAIN} is stronger and used only at large amplitude.
          The source says plainly it is \textbf{not independently validated}.
    \item $K_i$ and $K_d$ share each channel's \emph{sign}, because they share
          one loop --- but not its \emph{magnitude}: ch2 runs
          $|K_i| = 0.040$ like the rest while its $|K_p|$ is $0.010$.
    \item \texttt{I\_CLAMP\_V} $= 0.15$ is ``only a backstop''. The real
          anti-windup is back-calculation at \texttt{TRACK\_TC\_S} $= 0.5$\,s.
  \end{itemize}}
  \vspace{1mm}
  {\scriptsize\textit{Sources: comment block above \texttt{STEADY\_GAIN} in
  \texttt{osem.v9.py}; \texttt{provenance.md} Table 1 and \S\,``What the report
  does NOT contain''.}}
\end{frame}
\note{Now the honest part: how were these numbers chosen?

Minus 0.030 on channel zero is the only gain in this entire repository that was
ever watched on an oscilloscope. That was 2026-07-15, run three: real damping,
a six-fold reduction in amplitude, no rail clipping. Everything else is either
copied from that, inferred from a report, or guessed conservatively.

The capture gain is a two-level schedule -- stronger while the amplitude is
still large, tapering to steady as it comes down. The source comment states
flatly that capture is not independently validated. It is minus 0.035, which is
deliberately just under the minus 0.040 limit we will come to in two slides.

Ki and Kd follow each channel's sign because all three terms drive the same
coil through the same loop. But note that their magnitudes do not track Kp:
channel two runs Ki of 0.040 like everyone else while its Kp is only 0.010,
four times smaller. Nothing in the repo justifies that asymmetry -- it is worth
knowing before anyone retunes.

Finally the integrator clamp. It is 0.15 volts, and the source calls it only a
backstop. The mechanism that actually stops windup is back-calculation with a
half-second tracking constant, added in v3. On the bench the clamp never even
engaged: peak I was 0.084 volts against that 0.150 limit.}

\note{Channel two is the story that keeps paying off.

Table 1 of the original report fits an exponential decay to each channel with
all four damping together. A2 has the largest decay constant of the four --
0.2796 per second, a time constant of 3.58 seconds -- and Figure 2's
applied-gain panel shows it running at plus 0.010 while the others ran at minus
0.030. A positive gain on a correctly-wired channel would pump the resonance,
not damp it. The only consistent reading is that channel two's coil or its OSEM
is physically mounted the other way round, so its loop sign is inverted.

This was confirmed on the bench on 2026-08-04, on the first four-channel run
after the rewiring: channel two ran positive while the other three ran negative
and it came out the best-damping axis of the four, ratio 0.01 to 0.03 against
channel zero's 0.08.

The R-squared values behind those fits are 0.65 to 0.92, so treat one
significant figure as the honest precision. The simulator carries the same fact
as a negative coil gain, about four times the magnitude of the others, which is
why a gain three times smaller still damps fastest.}

\note{Every gain in this project rests on a limit that has almost no evidence
behind it.

The claim is that minus 0.030 damps cleanly and minus 0.040 is the onset of
instability or railing. The entire record of that is one undated sentence in
the docstring of v0, a file that has since been deleted and lives only in git
history. The weekly report, written two days later, does not mention it. No CSV
in the data directory records it. And the simulator cannot help, because the
plant it models is linear apart from the ADC: sweep the gain to minus 0.600 and
damping just increases monotonically with zero faults.

There is even a counter-indication. That measurement was taken with a single
channel driving. v1 then ran all four channels at minus 0.030, which is four
times the total actuation authority, without any trouble. So whatever minus
0.040 is, it is not a simple total-authority effect.

The right posture is the one the README takes: treat it as real and
uncharacterised. Do not go past minus 0.035 unattended. Research item 3 is the
bench sweep that would settle it -- keep the twelve-column CSV and a scope
trace, and separate where clipping starts from where oscillation grows. The
leading hypothesis is phase lag from the finite-difference velocity estimator.}

\begin{frame}{The gain schedule, and the baseline everything scales off}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.53\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      \begin{align*}
        \mathrm{RMS}_{\mathrm{base}} &= \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum \mathrm{bp}(t_i)^2}
          \quad \text{at gain} = 0 \\
        r(t) &= \mathrm{RMS}_{\mathrm{win}}[\mathrm{bp}(t)] \big/ \mathrm{RMS}_{\mathrm{base}} \\
        K(t) &= K_{\mathrm{steady}} + s(r)\,(K_{\mathrm{cap}} - K_{\mathrm{steady}}) \\
        s(r) &= \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\tfrac{r-0.2}{0.4},\,0,\,1\right)
      \end{align*}

      \vspace{3mm}
      {\footnotesize
      Slew-limited at \texttt{GAIN\_SLEW\_PER\_S} $=0.02$, so there is no gain
      step that could itself kick a transient. That ramp doubles as the startup
      soft-start: $0 \to$ capture gain in 1.75\,s.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.45\textwidth}
      \centering
      \begin{tikzpicture}
        \begin{axis}[width=5.8cm, height=4.0cm,
                     xlabel={ratio $r$ (amplitude / own baseline)},
                     ylabel={$|K|$}, scaled y ticks=false,
                     xmin=0, xmax=1.0, ymin=0.028, ymax=0.037,
                     axis lines=left, ytick={0.030,0.035},
                     yticklabels={$0.030$, $0.035$}]
          \addplot[thick, samples=200, domain=0:1]
            {0.030 + 0.005*max(0,min(1,(x-0.2)/0.4))};
          \draw[dashed] (axis cs:0.35,0.028) -- (axis cs:0.35,0.037);
          \node[font=\scriptsize, anchor=west] at (axis cs:0.36,0.0295) {lock line 0.35};
        \end{axis}
      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{2mm}
  \centering\footnotesize
  \textbf{One number, three jobs:} the runaway breaker trips above
  $1.8\times$ baseline, the scheduler blends between $0.2\times$ and
  $0.6\times$, and \textbf{\texttt{LOCKED} means below $0.35\times$ held for 5\,s}.
\end{frame}
\note{Two more pieces of the baseline law.

First, the gain schedule. Rather than run one fixed gain, each channel blends
between its steady gain and its stronger capture gain according to how large
the oscillation currently is relative to that channel's own calibrated
baseline. Full capture above 0.6 of baseline, tapering linearly to steady by
0.2, and every change slew-limited at 0.02 per second so the gain change cannot
itself kick the optic. That same ramp is the startup soft-start: it takes about
1.75 seconds to reach capture gain from zero.

Second, and this is the load-bearing part: baseline RMS. It is measured once
per calibration with the gain forced to zero, and then three different things
scale off it. The runaway breaker trips above 1.8 times baseline. The scheduler
blends between 0.2 and 0.6 times baseline. And the lock detector -- which
produces the run's actual deliverable, the LOCKED message -- fires below 0.35
times baseline held for five seconds.

That is a lot of weight on one number measured in one window, and it is the
reason four separate versions later spend their effort on when and how that
number is measured.}

\note{The four OSEMs are not four independent oscillators. They are four

degrees of freedom and are strongly correlated by construction.

With four corners and three rigid-body freedoms -- longitudinal, pitch and yaw
-- there is a fourth combination, called butterfly, that no rigid-body motion
can produce. Any signal there is a sensor disagreeing with the other three,
which is a free consistency check that does not depend on any threshold in ADC
counts. The simulator models the plant this way.

Two measured facts follow, and both are load-bearing. Driving a single
OSEM-coil pair damps all four channels, because removing energy from one
projection removes it from the coupled system -- about seventy seconds for the
whole system. Running all four independent loops together settles it in about
seventeen. That four-fold improvement is the entire justification for v1
existing, and the seventy-second single-channel result is what later justifies
letting the rig keep running on one healthy axis.

Note carefully what this is not: it is still four independent single-input
single-output loops. Each OSEM only ever drives its own coil. There is no
decoupling between modes anywhere in v0 through v10.}

\note{One string variable, three values, and it gates actuation and is written
to every CSV row. The simulator drives the shipping code through this same
state machine rather than reimplementing it.

Calibrating: all gains forced to zero, outputs held at bias, accumulating the
squared bandpassed signal. On exit it sets baseline RMS, which as we saw is the
reference for the runaway breaker, the scheduler and the lock detector.

Damping: the only state that actuates. Per sample it schedules gains, checks
for runaway, updates the lock detector, and actuates.

Fault: all outputs to bias, gain zeroed every sample, waiting for five seconds
of all-clear before re-calibrating.

Two structural notes. There is no way out of DAMPING except into FAULT, and
there is no terminal state -- Ctrl-C leaves through a finally block that
returns every DAC channel to bias and closes the log. And note what does not
run in FAULT: the schedule update, the runaway check and the lock update are
all skipped. That omission is the first defect we will look at.}

      trip recovered fine; \textbf{only saturation deadlocked}.

      \vspace{2mm}
      \textbf{The fix (v3).} Maintain the flag in \texttt{actuate()}, where the
      output is actually known --- so it refreshes every sample in every state,
      including \texttt{FAULT}, where the output is held at bias and it clears
      on its own.

      \vspace{2mm}
      \alert{\textbf{Reproduced on hardware, 2026-08-03.}} Across 16{,}966
      \texttt{FAULT} samples v2 logged exactly \textbf{one distinct ratio value
      (3.6803)} --- nothing was being recomputed at all.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}
\note{The first of the three original defects, and the only one reproduced on
hardware.

v0 recomputed the saturated flag only inside check-runaway, and check-runaway
does not run in the FAULT state. So a saturation trip would set the flag, enter
FAULT, and then nothing could ever clear it: a permanent fault requiring a
manual restart, in a module that advertises auto-recovery. A runaway trip
recovered fine, because it never set that flag. Only saturation deadlocked.

The fix in v3 is to maintain the flag in actuate, where the output is actually
known, so it refreshes every sample in every state including FAULT -- and in
FAULT the output is held at bias, so it clears itself.

What makes this the strongest defect story is the evidence. On the bench, v2
latched for the last fifty seconds of a run, and the proof that it was the
stale flag rather than a live condition is that across 16,966 FAULT samples
there is exactly one distinct ratio value, 3.6803. Nothing was being recomputed
at all.}

\begin{frame}{The v4 that failed: modal (MIMO) damping}
  \begin{columns}[T]

\note{This is the story beat I most wanted in the deck.

There were two different files called osem.v4.py. This is the first one:
a modal, MIMO controller, written and deleted on the same day, 2026-08-03.

The idea is genuinely better than what shipped. Four independent loops on one
rigid body is the wrong decomposition -- each loop is fighting a plant the
other three are also driving. So: learn the modes from the covariance of the
sensor vector during calibration, learn the actuation matrix by dithering each
coil in turn, damp per mode, and distribute the modal command through the

notably, was fine -- 98.8 percent of the energy in one mode.

supervisor fixes.

then re-calibrate. That is twenty seconds of open loop with the gain at zero.
It is exactly right for a transient. It is exactly wrong for a disturbance that
is still present, because during those twenty seconds the optic rings straight
back up to the amplitude that faulted it, and faults again on contact. The
result is a supervisor-level feedback loop that spends its life measuring
instead of damping.

The numbers are dramatic. Over two hundred seconds of sustained clipping, v3
and v4 spend four percent of samples actually damping across seven faults and
never lock. v5 spends 87.5 percent and locks in ten seconds.

re-engage on the baseline already in hand rather than measuring a new one. It
is bounded at four reuses so a stale baseline cannot persist. And note how it
was found: by stressing the rebuilt simulator across clipping regimes, not by
reading the code.

What it does not fix: at the highest drive, with 56 percent of samples
clipping, v5 still exhausts its reuses. That regime is a sensor problem, not a
control problem.}

\note{This is the best single result in the project, and it deserves three

The runaway breaker, from v0 all the way through v8, was a level test: is the
envelope above 1.8 times baseline, sustained for two seconds. That sounds
reasonable and it is wrong, for a reason that is obvious once stated.

growing. A large but decaying envelope means the loop is succeeding. A level
test cannot distinguish those two. So after a kick, while the optic is ringing

happens to fall under the line by luck.

\begin{frame}{What the simulator can and cannot tell you}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      \textbf{It runs the \emph{real} controller} --- every four-channel
      version is imported and stepped, not reimplemented. Only \texttt{serial}
      and \texttt{DACController} are stubbed.

      \vspace{2mm}
      The plant is a linear second-order oscillator per mode with
      \textbf{exactly one nonlinearity}: the ADC, which offsets each channel to
      its measured resting count, rounds to an integer, and hard-clips at
      0/1023. That is why clipping emerges on its own --- and with the bench's
      asymmetry.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      \alert{\textbf{What it cannot tell you:}}
      \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
        \item \textbf{A safe gain.} More negative $K_p$ is monotonically more
              damping all the way to $-0.6$. The ADC model reproduces
              \emph{clipping}, not instability.
        \item \textbf{A wrong coil map.} \texttt{sim/server.py} round-trips its
              held-voltage dict through whatever map the controller declares.
              \textbf{Only the bench catches one.}
      \end{itemize}

      \vspace{2mm}
      And \texttt{COIL\_GAIN = [1.00, 0.76, $-$4.15, 0.86]} is \emph{inferred}
      from Table 1, not measured --- the report characterises no actuator.
      \textbf{One significant figure is the honest precision.}
      $Q$ and drive amplitude are sliders because neither was ever
      characterised.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}
\note{A slide about epistemics, because it is what keeps the numbers in this
deck trustworthy.

The simulator's strength is that it runs the real controller. Every
four-channel version in the repository is imported and stepped, not
reimplemented. Only the serial module and the DAC controller are stubbed. So a
behavioural result in the simulator is a result about the code that will run on
the bench.

The plant is a linear second-order oscillator per mode with exactly one
nonlinearity -- the ADC. It offsets each channel to its measured resting count,
rounds to an integer, and hard-clips. Which is why clipping emerges on its own
in simulation, and with the bench's actual asymmetry: every OSEM rests high, so
the top rail comes first, and channel two hits it first of all.

But it has two blind spots that no amount of simulation closes. It cannot tell
you a safe gain, because the plant is linear and damping just increases
monotonically to minus 0.6. And it cannot catch a wrong coil map, because it
round-trips its held-voltage dictionary through whatever map the controller
declares.

Finally, the coil gains it uses are inferred from the report's decay table by
subtracting the intrinsic decay and dividing by the applied gain -- with the
simulator's assumed Q. The report characterises no actuator at all. One
significant figure is the honest precision, and Q and drive amplitude are
sliders precisely because nobody measured them.}
