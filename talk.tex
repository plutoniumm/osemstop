\begin{frame}{The pendulum, and why it needs help}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      A suspended optic is a damped harmonic oscillator. For one sensed
      coordinate $x(t)$, of mass $m$, driven by force $F(t)$:
      \[ \ddot{x} + \frac{\omega_0}{Q}\,\dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = \frac{F(t)}{m} \]
      \vspace{-3mm}
      \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
        \item $\omega_0 = 2\pi f_0$, the resonance
        \item $Q$, the quality factor
        \item amplitude decays as $e^{-\gamma t}$ with
              $\gamma = \omega_0 / 2Q$, and $\tau \equiv 1/\gamma$
      \end{itemize}

      \vspace{2mm}
      \textbf{Measured, 320\,s ringdown, coils held at bias:}

      \vspace{1mm}
      \centering
      {\scriptsize
      \begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
        \toprule
        $f_A$ & $0.7155 \pm 0.0001$\,Hz \\
        $f_B$ & $0.9949 \pm 0.0001$\,Hz \\
        $f_C$ & $1.6396 \pm 0.0001$\,Hz \\
        \midrule
        $\gamma$ & $+0.00004 \pm 0.00719$\,s$^{-1}$ \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
      \footnotesize\raggedright
      \textbf{Three resonances}, all inside the 0.4--3\,Hz band the loop reads.

      \vspace{2mm}
      \textbf{The measured $\gamma$ is consistent with zero.} So $\gamma$ gives
      a \emph{bound}, not a value:
      \[ Q > 433\ (1\sigma), \qquad \tau > 138\,\mathrm{s} \]

      \vspace{2mm}
      \alert{\textbf{That is why it needs help.}} A suspension is built to be
      lossless, so a kick rings for \emph{minutes}. With the loop closed the
      same kick settles in \textbf{2.9--4.7\,s}:
      \[ \frac{\tau_{\mathrm{open}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{closed}}} > 29 \]
      \textbf{That ratio is the entire reason the loop exists.}

      \vspace{2mm}
      {\scriptsize A $\tau \approx 16$\,s figure is still carried in the
      simulator. It comes from an assumed $Q = 50$, which this ringdown excludes
      at \textbf{8.7$\sigma$}.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}
\note{The plant, measured from a 320 second quiet ringdown with the coils held
at bias.

A suspended optic is a damped harmonic oscillator. One coordinate x, resonance
omega-nought equals two pi f-nought, quality factor Q. The amplitude decays as e
to the minus gamma t, where gamma is omega-nought over two Q, and tau is one over
gamma. Those symbols are used the same way for the rest of the deck.

Three resonances: 0.7155, 0.9949 and 1.6396 hertz, each to a tenth of a
millihertz. All three sit inside the 0.4 to 3 hertz band the loop closes on,
which matters because a resonance outside that band would be filtered out of the
signal the loop uses.

The measured decay rate is consistent with zero. So it gives a bound rather than
a value: Q greater than 433 at one sigma, tau greater than 138 seconds.

And that is the whole answer to why this needs a loop. A suspension is built to
be lossless, so a kick rings for minutes. With the loop closed the same kick
settles in three to five seconds. The ratio is at least twenty-nine, and that
ratio is the entire reason the loop exists.

One warning worth keeping: a sixteen second figure is still carried in the
simulator, and it comes from an assumed Q of fifty, which this measurement
excludes at 8.7 sigma.}

\begin{frame}{Cold damping: the process variable is \emph{velocity}}
  \begin{align*}
    \mathrm{bp}(t) &= \mathrm{LP}_{3\,\mathrm{Hz}}\big[\,\mathrm{HP}_{0.4\,\mathrm{Hz}}[A(t)]\,\big] \\[2pt]
    v(t) &= \mathrm{LP}_{5\,\mathrm{Hz}}\!\left[\frac{\mathrm{bp}(t)-\mathrm{bp}(t-\Delta t)}{\Delta t}\right] \\[2pt]
    u(t) &= -K(t)\cdot v(t)
  \end{align*}
  \vspace{-2mm}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=4mm]
    \node[blk] (osem) {OSEM\\counts};
    \node[blk, right=of osem] (bp) {bandpass\\0.4--3\,Hz};
    \node[blk, right=of bp] (dv) {$d/dt$ +\\LP 5\,Hz};
    \node[blk, right=of dv] (err) {$e = -v$\\setpoint 0};
    \node[blk, right=of err] (pid) {$K_p,K_i,K_d$};
    \node[blk, right=of pid] (dac) {clip to\\0--0.5\,V};
    \node[blk, right=of dac] (coil) {coil};
    \foreach \a/\b in {osem/bp, bp/dv, dv/err, err/pid, pid/dac, dac/coil}
      \draw[flow] (\a) -- (\b);
    \draw[flow] (coil.south) -- ++(0,-0.55) -| node[pos=0.25, below, font=\scriptsize]
      {optic} (osem.south);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{1mm}
  {\footnotesize The setpoint is a constant zero, so $e = -v$. The loop does not
  ask where the optic \emph{is}, only that it stop \emph{moving}. All three
  measured modes sit inside 0.4--3\,Hz.}
\end{frame}
\note{The whole control law, unchanged since v2. Bandpass the raw sensor between
0.4 and 3 hertz, differentiate to get velocity, smooth at 5 hertz, drive the coil
with minus a gain times velocity.

The critical thing, and what makes textbook intuition misleading here, is that
the process variable is velocity and the setpoint is a constant zero. So the
error is simply minus the velocity. The loop has no opinion about where the optic
sits; it only objects to it moving. That is cold damping: synthesising a viscous
drag the suspension deliberately does not have.

The bandpass matters. All three measured modes sit inside 0.4 to 3 hertz. A mode
outside that band would be filtered out of the very signal the loop closes on.}

\begin{frame}{Three gains: P is a damper, I is a spring, D is a mass}
  \centering
  With $e = -v$ and $v = \dot{x}$:
  \[
    \underbrace{K_p\,e = -K_p\,\dot{x}}_{\text{force} \propto \text{velocity}}
    \qquad
    \underbrace{K_i\!\int\! e\,dt = -K_i\,x}_{\text{force} \propto \text{displacement}}
    \qquad
    \underbrace{K_d\,\frac{de}{dt} = -K_d\,\ddot{x}}_{\text{force} \propto \text{acceleration}}
  \]
  \vspace{1mm}
  {\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
    \toprule
    term & acts on & physically & effect on the pole \\
    \midrule
    $K_p$ & velocity & \textbf{viscous damper} & \textbf{the only term that removes energy} \\
    $K_i$ & displacement & added \textbf{spring} & moves the resonance; does not damp \\
    $K_d$ & acceleration & added (negative) \textbf{mass} & noisiest; differentiates twice \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}}

  \vspace{3mm}
  \begin{minipage}{0.9\textwidth}
  \footnotesize
  \textbf{Why add I and D at all?} $K_p$ sets the damping, but it leaves a steady
  offset it cannot remove and it reacts only to present error. $K_i$ takes out the
  offset; $K_d$ anticipates. Neither dissipates, so
  \alert{\textbf{amplitude ratio is the wrong metric to expect an improvement
  in}}.

  \vspace{1.5mm}
  Measured: switching $K_i$ and $K_d$ on moved the \textbf{ch0 amplitude ratio}
  from \textbf{0.025} to \textbf{0.028}, which is no improvement.
  \end{minipage}
\end{frame}
\note{The most illuminating slide in the deck for anyone who knows ordinary PID.

Because the error is minus velocity, every term is shifted by one derivative from
what its name suggests. P acts on velocity, so it is a viscous damper, and it is
the only one of the three that takes energy out of the pendulum. I integrates
velocity, and the integral of velocity is displacement, so I is a spring. D
differentiates velocity, and the derivative of velocity is acceleration, so D is
added mass, and having differentiated the sensor twice it is by far the noisiest
term.

Now the question that slide usually leaves hanging: why add I and D at all? P
sets the damping, but P alone leaves a steady offset it cannot remove, and it
reacts only to the error in front of it. I takes out the offset, D anticipates.
Neither dissipates energy. So the amplitude ratio is the wrong number to expect
them to improve, and when v2 measured 0.028 against v1's 0.025 that was the
predicted result, not a disappointment.

And the measured consequence: switching Ki and Kd on moved the channel zero
amplitude ratio from 0.025 to 0.028. No improvement, which is what the physics
predicts.}

\begin{frame}{The velocity gains, and what set them}
  \footnotesize
  \textbf{The loop feeds back velocity.} For OSEM $i$, $\hat{v}_i$ is its
  bandpassed signal differentiated, in \textbf{V/s}; $u_i$ is the voltage added
  to that channel's coil bias, in \textbf{V}. The proportional term is
  \[ u_i = -K_p^{(i)}\,\hat{v}_i, \qquad\text{so } K_p \text{ has units V per V/s.} \]
  \vspace{-2mm}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \centering
      {\footnotesize
      \begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc|c@{}}
        \toprule
        & ch0 & ch1 & \textbf{ch2} & ch3 & ch4--7 \\
        \midrule
        $K_p$ & $-0.035$ & $-0.035$ & $\mathbf{+0.035}$ & $-0.035$ & $0$ \\
        $K_i$ & $-0.0060$ & $-0.0060$ & $\mathbf{+0.0060}$ & $-0.0060$ & $0$ \\
        $K_d$ & $-0.00045$ & $-0.00045$ & $\mathbf{+0.00045}$ & $-0.00045$ & $0$ \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}}

      \vspace{2mm}
      \raggedright
      $K_p$ is \textbf{negative} because the coil force must \emph{oppose}
      velocity.

      \vspace{1.5mm}
      \alert{\textbf{ch2 is positive and that is correct, not a typo.}} Its OSEM
      and coil are mounted the other way round, so its loop sign is inverted:
      $K_p > 0$ there opposes velocity exactly as $K_p < 0$ does elsewhere.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
      \raggedright
      \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2.5pt}
        \item \textbf{$|K_p| = 0.030$ was set against an oscilloscope} on ch0,
              watching the sensor trace directly: \textbf{6$\times$ amplitude
              reduction, no rail clipping}. It is the only value in the project
              fixed that way.
        \item \alert{\textbf{Bound: $|K_p| = 0.040$ is the measured onset of
              instability and railing.}} \textbf{Do not exceed $0.035$
              unattended.} That is where the shipping value sits.
        \item \textbf{$K_i$ and $K_d$ take each channel's sign}, because all
              three terms drive one coil through one loop. Their
              \emph{magnitudes} are set by the actuator budget: peak coil demand
              \textbf{0.2001\,V} inside a $\pm 0.25$\,V window.
        \item \textbf{ch4--ch7 run at zero} because their coil sign is not
              determined, and a wrong-signed channel \emph{pumps}.
        \item Anti-windup is back-calculation at \texttt{TRACK\_TC\_S} $= 0.5$\,s.
              \texttt{I\_CLAMP\_V} $= 0.15$\,V is a backstop: measured peak
              $|I|$ is \textbf{0.084\,V}, so it has never engaged.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}
\note{The gains, as they ship.

First the units, because a gain without units is not a number. v-hat is the
bandpassed sensor signal differentiated, in volts per second. u is the voltage
added to that channel's coil bias, in volts. So Kp is volts per volt-per-second,
which is dimensionally seconds.

Kp is negative because the coil force has to oppose velocity. That is the whole
mechanism: a force proportional to minus velocity is a dashpot.

Channel two is positive, and it is correct rather than a typo. Its OSEM and its
coil are mounted the other way round, so the loop sign is inverted, and a
positive Kp there opposes velocity exactly as a negative one does everywhere
else. The next slide is the measurement behind that claim.

Now how the numbers were set. A magnitude of 0.030 was fixed on channel zero
against an oscilloscope, watching the sensor trace: six-fold amplitude reduction
with no rail clipping. It is the only value in this project ever fixed that way.

The bound is 0.040, which is the measured onset of instability and railing. The
shipping value sits just under it at 0.035, and nothing should exceed that
unattended.

Ki and Kd take each channel's sign because all three terms drive one coil
through one loop. Their magnitudes are set by the actuator budget rather than by
tuning for damping: peak coil demand comes to 0.2001 volts inside a plus or minus
0.25 volt window.

Channels four to seven run at zero gain because their coil sign is not
determined, and a wrong-signed channel pumps its mode rather than damping it.

And the integrator clamp is a backstop that has never engaged: measured peak
integrator output is 0.084 volts against a 0.150 volt limit.}

\note{Channel two is the story that keeps paying off.

The v0 campaign fits an exponential decay to each channel with all four damping
together. A2 has the largest decay constant of the four, 0.2796 per second, and
it was running at plus 0.010 while the others ran at minus 0.030. A positive gain
on a correctly-wired channel would pump the resonance, not damp it. The only
consistent reading is that channel two's coil or OSEM is mounted the other way
round.

It has now been confirmed three separate ways. The decay fit. A four-channel
bench run where ch2 came out the best-damping axis. And a fixed-frequency refit
that put ch2's residue at plus 13.573 against roughly minus 8 for the others, in
both modes rather than one. So the most effective channel of the four was also
the most under-driven, which is where the diagonal-reallocation item on the open
list comes from.

The R-squared values behind the decay fits are 0.65 to 0.92, so one significant
figure is the honest precision.}

\begin{frame}{The gain schedule, and the baseline everything scales off}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.53\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      \begin{align*}
        \mathrm{RMS}_{\mathrm{base}} &= \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum \mathrm{bp}(t_i)^2}
          \quad \text{at gain} = 0 \\
        r(t) &= \mathrm{RMS}_{\mathrm{win}}[\mathrm{bp}(t)] \big/ \mathrm{RMS}_{\mathrm{base}} \\
        K(t) &= K_{\mathrm{steady}} + s(r)\,(K_{\mathrm{cap}} - K_{\mathrm{steady}}) \\
        s(r) &= \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\tfrac{r-0.2}{0.4},\,0,\,1\right)
      \end{align*}

      \vspace{2mm}
      {\footnotesize Slew-limited at \texttt{GAIN\_SLEW\_PER\_S} $= 0.02$, so no
      gain step can itself kick a transient. The same ramp is the startup
      soft-start: $0 \to$ capture gain in 1.75\,s.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.45\textwidth}
      \centering
      \begin{tikzpicture}

\note{The third defect, and the one that was never really fixed.

Start with the data, because it makes the cost concrete. Three runs on the same
rig on the same afternoon calibrated channel zero's floor at 0.6316, 0.3158 and
0.1804 volts. Everything downstream is a ratio against that number, so the
runaway trip line moves from 1.137 volts to 0.325, and the lock line from 0.221

motion v3 required.

\begin{frame}{The v4 that failed: modal (MIMO) damping}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      \textbf{The idea, and it is a good one.} Four loops each fighting a plant
      the other three are also driving is the wrong decomposition. Control the
      \emph{motion}, not the sensors:
      \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
        \item Accumulate the $4\times4$ covariance during calibration. Its

        \item Damp per \emph{mode}; distribute through $B^{+}$.

      the other way instead of having to be told.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}

The idea is better than what shipped. Four independent loops on one rigid body is
the wrong decomposition. So: learn the modes from the covariance of the sensor

The plant is a linear second-order oscillator per mode with exactly one
nonlinearity, the ADC. That is why clipping emerges on its own in simulation,
with the bench's actual asymmetry.

The blind spots. It cannot tell you a safe gain, because the plant is linear and
damping increases monotonically to minus 0.6. It cannot catch a wrong coil map,

controller declares. And it could not have found the torn serial frame, because
